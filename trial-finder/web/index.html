Common signs of a predatory or pay-to-participate trial

Our oversight checklist above is built from structured facts in each trial's public record — sponsor type, FDA regulation, safety monitoring, and so on. It can't tell you whether a treatment itself is scientifically sound; a trial can look clean on every one of those facts and still be testing something unproven. These are patterns to watch for yourself, in addition to our checklist, not instead of it.

Cost

  • You're asked to pay for the experimental treatment itself — not incidental costs like parking, but the actual intervention being studied. Legitimate research typically doesn't charge participants for this.
  • The price is unusually high, or framed as a limited-time offer pushing you to decide quickly.

What's offered as evidence

  • Patient testimonials are the main thing offered — not published results or data you can check yourself.
  • The same treatment is marketed for many unrelated conditions.
  • Heavy advertising, rather than the kind of understated, technical language most legitimate trial listings use.

Oversight and the people involved

  • Ask whether the treatment is under an active FDA Investigational New Drug (IND) or Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) application — and ask to see the approval letter. A listing on ClinicalTrials.gov does not by itself mean the FDA has reviewed it.
  • Ask where the cells, drug, or device came from, what was done to it, and exactly how it's supposed to help you — and get the answers in writing.
  • Check who actually performs the procedure (a licensed physician, versus unlicensed staff).
  • Using your own cells or tissue isn't automatically safe — real risks (infection, the treatment simply not working, abnormal growths) exist either way.
